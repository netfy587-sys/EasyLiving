The bike is simple — what you do with it isn't

A page on how road cycling actually works — the parts of the bike, how gearing changes effort, and how training zones turn a ride into something measurable.

Where the effort actually goes

A road bike converts leg power into forward motion through a chain of very simple mechanisms — but small changes in any of them change how a ride feels enormously. Tire pressure, gear choice, and body position all move the needle more than most riders expect.

Cadence — how fast the pedals turn — matters as much as gear choice. Most efficient riders hold somewhere around 85 to 95 revolutions per minute, adjusting gears rather than slowing their spin on hills.

Four parts worth understanding

Frame

Geometry sets the bike's handling — a longer wheelbase is stable, a shorter one is quick to turn.

Drivetrain

Chain, chainrings, and cassette together — this is what actually turns pedal effort into speed.

Wheels

Rim depth and spoke count trade off between weight, stiffness, and how the bike handles crosswind.

Brakes

Disc brakes give more consistent stopping power in wet conditions than traditional rim brakes.

Five training zones

  • Zone 1 — Recovery<60%

    Easy spinning, used between hard efforts or on rest days.

  • Zone 2 — Endurance60–70%

    Conversational pace — most base training happens here.

  • Zone 3 — Tempo70–80%

    Comfortably hard, sustainable for an hour or more.

  • Zone 4 — Threshold80–90%

    The pace you could hold for roughly one hour, all out.

  • Zone 5 — VO2 max90–100%

    Short, hard intervals — a few minutes at a time, at most.

Riding in a group

  • Hold your line

    Predictable positioning matters more in a group than individual speed — sudden swerves cause the crashes.

  • Call out hazards

    Potholes, gravel, and cars are usually announced verbally and with a hand signal to riders behind.

  • Take a fair turn

    Riders at the front rotate through, sharing the work of blocking wind for the group.
